The Technique

What is Scanning Tunneling Microscopy?

Scanning tunneling microscopy (STM) is a powerful technique for imaging surfaces at the atomic scale. Invented in 1981 by Gerd Binnig and Heinrich Rohrer — for which they received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1986 — STM works by exploiting the quantum mechanical phenomenon of electron tunneling.

A sharp metallic tip is brought within a few ångströms of a conducting surface. When a bias voltage is applied, electrons tunnel through the vacuum gap between tip and sample, generating a measurable current that is exquisitely sensitive to the tip-surface distance and to the local electronic structure.

By scanning the tip across the surface and recording the tunneling current, we can construct real-space images of the atomic landscape and map the local density of electronic states — a technique called scanning tunneling spectroscopy (STS).

STM schematic diagram

Atomic Resolution

STM can resolve individual atoms on a surface, achieving spatial resolutions below 0.1 nm — revealing the true atomic structure of materials.

Electronic Spectroscopy

By measuring tunneling current as a function of voltage (dI/dV), we map the local density of states with meV energy resolution.

Quasiparticle Interference

Elastic scattering of electrons off impurities creates standing waves we can image and use to reconstruct the Fermi surface and band structure.

What STM Can Achieve

Probing Quantum Phenomena at the Atomic Scale

Topological Materials

STM can directly image the hallmark signatures of topological matter — surface states, Dirac cones, Majorana-bound states — in real space and energy space simultaneously, providing definitive evidence that bulk topological invariants predict surface phenomena.
